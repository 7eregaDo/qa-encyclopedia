## The Intent
The interviewer is checking whether you treat privacy as a testable quality dimension, not just a legal obligation. Anonymisation pipelines are code — they have bugs. A field missed by a regex, a hash function that preserves too much structure, or a quasi-identifier combination that enables re-identification are all defects a QA engineer can and should find.

## The STAR Script
**Situation:** My team was building a pipeline to anonymise customer transaction records before they were exported to a third-party analytics vendor. The pipeline was supposed to remove names and email addresses, pseudonymise customer IDs with a one-way hash, and generalise postcodes from full postcode to district level (e.g., "SW1A 2AA" → "SW1A"). No tests existed for the anonymisation logic.

**Task:** I was asked to design a test suite that would verify the anonymisation was correct, complete, and sufficient.

**Action:** I structured the tests across three dimensions. Dimension 1 — direct identifier removal: I wrote tests verifying that the output dataset contained no values matching patterns for direct identifiers. I used regex patterns to scan for email addresses (@ sign pattern), UK phone numbers, National Insurance numbers, and full postcodes. I also verified the `name` and `email` columns were entirely absent from the output schema — not just empty. I tested the edge cases: names embedded in free-text fields (a `notes` column that sometimes contained "spoke to John Smith about..."), and email addresses in unexpected fields (a `contact_preference` field that some records used to store an email address). Dimension 2 — pseudonymisation correctness: I verified the customer ID hash was one-way (I could not reverse it to the original ID), consistent (the same customer ID always produced the same hash), and collision-free in the test dataset (no two different customer IDs produced the same hash). I also verified the hash was salted — an unsalted hash of a known identifier space (account numbers follow a predictable format) can be brute-forced. Dimension 3 — re-identification risk: I performed a quasi-identifier analysis on the output dataset — combining postcode district, age band, and transaction date, I assessed whether any combination uniquely identified a small number of individuals. I found 23 records where the combination of postcode district (very specific rural area), age, and transaction date was unique — potentially re-identifiable. The postcode generalisation was extended to area level for low-population postcode districts.

**Result:** The free-text field scan found 312 records containing customer names in the `notes` column — a gap the development team had not anticipated. The unsalted hash issue was a significant finding: the team had not known hashing without a salt was reversible for structured identifier spaces. Both were fixed. The re-identification analysis was added as a monthly audit run against the anonymised export dataset.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Direct identifier** | An attribute that directly identifies an individual — such as name, email address, national ID number, or full postcode |
| **Quasi-identifier** | An attribute that does not directly identify an individual but can be combined with other attributes to enable re-identification — such as age, gender, and postcode |
| **Pseudonymisation** | The replacement of direct identifiers with tokens or hashes — reducing direct identification risk while retaining the ability to link records to the same individual |
| **Salted hash** | A cryptographic hash computed with an additional random value (the salt) — preventing brute-force reversal by making the output unpredictable even for known input spaces |
| **Re-identification risk** | The probability that an individual can be identified from an anonymised dataset using the remaining attributes — the central metric for anonymisation sufficiency |
| **Quasi-identifier analysis** | An assessment of which combinations of non-identifying attributes could uniquely identify individuals in a dataset — used to determine whether additional generalisation is needed |
| **Data generalisation** | The replacement of a specific value with a less precise category — such as replacing an exact age with an age band, or a full postcode with a district — reducing re-identification risk |
