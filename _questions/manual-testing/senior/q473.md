## The Intent
The interviewer is checking whether you treat usability as a testable quality dimension — not just a UX designer's concern. At senior level, they want to see that you can design and run usability testing with rigour: defined tasks, representative participants, structured observation, and a findings classification system that enables the team to make informed decisions about remediation priority.

## The STAR Script
**Situation:** My organisation was releasing a new self-service account management portal for business customers. The design team had conducted wireframe reviews but no usability testing with real users. The product manager had flagged concerns that the account hierarchy management feature — which allowed users to create and manage sub-accounts — was complex and might confuse users. I was asked to design a usability testing programme within a two-week window before release.

**Task:** Design and run a structured usability testing programme that identified genuine usability problems in time to address the most critical ones before release.

**Action:** I structured the programme across four components. Component 1 — scope and task definition: I identified the five most critical tasks a new user would need to complete successfully: create a sub-account, assign a user to a sub-account, set billing preferences, generate a usage report, and close a sub-account. For each task, I wrote a scenario — a realistic business context (not "click on X") that gave participants a goal without revealing the navigation path. Example: "You've just onboarded a new client subsidiary. Set up a separate sub-account for them, call it 'Northside Division', and assign your colleague Jane Smith as its administrator." Component 2 — participant recruitment: I recruited six participants — three who were existing customers of similar products (representative of the experienced user) and three who were new to this category (representative of the less-experienced user). Six participants is sufficient to identify the majority of usability patterns in a task-based study. I scheduled sessions for 60 minutes each, two per day across three days. Component 3 — session facilitation: I used the think-aloud protocol — participants were asked to narrate their thoughts as they worked. I observed and took notes without assisting; a second observer tracked time-on-task and hesitation points. I recorded sessions with participant consent for review by the design team. I used a structured observation sheet with columns for: task, participant action, hesitation/error, verbal comment, and observer note. Component 4 — findings synthesis and classification: after all six sessions, I consolidated observations and classified findings into three severity levels. Blocker: task could not be completed by the majority of participants without assistance (2 findings). Significant: task was completed but with notable confusion, multiple attempts, or incorrect path (6 findings). Minor: small friction, cosmetic, or preference issue (11 findings). Each finding included a description, the supporting evidence (participant quotes and behaviours), and a recommended design action.

**Result:** Two blocker findings required redesign before release: the sub-account creation flow had no confirmation step before saving, and four of six participants assumed they had not saved because no confirmation appeared — two accidentally created duplicate sub-accounts. The billing preference screen used terminology from the internal system ("ledger centre") that none of the six participants recognised, causing all six to skip the step entirely. Both were redesigned and retested in a rapid three-participant follow-up. Release proceeded with 0 blocker usability issues. The findings report was incorporated into the design team's quarterly retrospective and three significant findings were addressed in the subsequent sprint.

## Seniority Shift

| Level | Expected Depth |
|-------|---------------|
| **Entry** | Can observe a user completing a task and note where they hesitate or make errors |
| **Mid** | Runs informal usability sessions for a specific feature, documents findings, and presents to the product team |
| **Senior** | Designs a complete usability testing programme — task definition, participant recruitment, session facilitation using think-aloud protocol, structured observation, findings synthesis, and severity classification — with a follow-up validation round for critical findings |

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Task-based usability testing** | A research method where participants are given realistic goal-based scenarios and observed attempting to complete them without guidance |
| **Think-aloud protocol** | An instruction for usability participants to narrate their thoughts as they work — providing insight into mental models, confusion, and expectations |
| **Blocker (usability)** | A usability finding where the majority of participants cannot complete the task without assistance — equivalent to a critical functional defect |
| **Scenario** | A realistic business context for a usability task that gives participants a goal without revealing the navigation path |
| **Time-on-task** | The elapsed time a participant takes to complete a task — a quantitative usability metric; tasks taking significantly longer than expected indicate friction |
| **Hesitation point** | A moment during task completion where a participant pauses, backtracks, or appears uncertain — a qualitative signal of usability friction |
| **Nielsen's rule of five** | The empirically supported finding that five to six participants in a usability study identify approximately 80% of usability problems — justifying small sample sizes for iterative testing |
| **Findings synthesis** | The process of consolidating observations across all participants into a coherent set of findings — identifying patterns rather than individual events |
