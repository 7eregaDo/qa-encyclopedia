## The Intent
The interviewer is checking whether you can translate legal obligations into engineering test cases — a skill that distinguishes senior QEs who can work across the law-engineering boundary from those who treat compliance as the legal team's responsibility. GDPR creates specific, testable technical requirements, and a QE who can verify them provides direct compliance value.

## The STAR Script
**Situation:** My organisation was launching a consumer application in the EU market. The legal team had produced a privacy notice and a DPA but had no visibility into whether the application's actual behaviour matched its stated privacy commitments. I was asked to design GDPR compliance testing to close this gap before launch.

**Task:** Design and execute a GDPR compliance test suite covering the key technical obligations — consent, data subject rights, data minimisation, retention, and breach detection capability.

**Action:** I mapped the regulation's technical requirements to five test domains. Domain 1 — consent: I verified that consent was collected before any personal data processing began — specifically, that no analytics or tracking scripts fired before the cookie banner was interacted with. I used the browser Network tab with the privacy extension uBlock Origin to observe which requests fired on page load before consent. I found two analytics providers loading before consent — a violation. I also tested consent withdrawal: after granting consent, I revoked it and verified the application stopped sending data to third-party analytics (verified via network requests) and deleted the consent cookie. Domain 2 — right to access (DSAR): I submitted a Data Subject Access Request through the application's DSAR form, acting as a test user. I verified: the response was provided within 30 days (we tested the workflow with a synthetic submission and measured the process time), the response included all data the application held on the user (I cross-referenced against the database directly), and the data was provided in a portable format (JSON export). Domain 3 — right to erasure: I requested deletion of a test account and verified: all personal data was physically deleted from the primary database, from backups created after the deletion request (the organisation's policy), from the analytics platform (confirmed via the analytics provider's API), and from the email marketing system. I found that deletion requests were not being propagated to the analytics platform — a compliance gap. Domain 4 — data minimisation: I reviewed every form and API endpoint that collected personal data and verified each field had a documented legal basis and was actually necessary for the stated purpose. I found two fields collected "for future use" with no current processing purpose — a minimisation violation. Domain 5 — retention enforcement: I tested that automated deletion jobs were running as scheduled and physically deleting records beyond their retention period — not just marking them as deleted (soft delete is insufficient under GDPR's right to erasure).

**Result:** Four compliance gaps found pre-launch: pre-consent analytics loading (critical — required immediate fix), deletion not propagated to analytics (high), minimisation violations (medium), and soft delete used instead of physical delete for the retention job (high). All were fixed before the EU launch. The DPO reviewed the test evidence and confirmed it satisfied the organisation's accountability obligations under Article 5(2).

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)** | EU regulation governing the collection, processing, and storage of personal data — with specific technical obligations enforceable through significant financial penalties |
| **Data Subject Access Request (DSAR)** | A formal request from an individual to receive a copy of all personal data held about them — organisations must respond within 30 days |
| **Right to erasure** | A GDPR right allowing individuals to request deletion of their personal data — requiring physical deletion from all systems, not merely soft deletion |
| **Consent withdrawal** | A GDPR requirement that individuals can withdraw consent as easily as they gave it — requiring cessation of processing and deletion of consent-dependent data |
| **Data minimisation** | A GDPR principle requiring that only the personal data strictly necessary for the stated purpose is collected and processed |
| **Accountability (Article 5(2))** | A GDPR principle requiring organisations to be able to demonstrate their compliance with the regulation — test evidence and documented processes satisfy this obligation |
| **Physical deletion** | The actual removal of personal data from storage, as opposed to soft deletion (flagging as deleted while retaining the data) — required for GDPR erasure compliance |
| **Pre-consent data collection** | The loading of tracking or analytics scripts before a user has granted consent — a direct violation of GDPR's requirement for lawful basis before processing |
